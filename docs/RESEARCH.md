# The research behind Intent

Every design decision in Intent traces back to a study, a well-documented practice, or an explicit judgement call. This page lists them, along with what each source does **not** show. Intent makes no medical claims: it can't "reset dopamine", treat ADHD or addiction, or make anyone smarter. What it can do is make the thing you meant to do easy, and the thing you didn't mean to do a little slower.

Most studies here are small, short, survey-based or uncontrolled, and effects of apps like this one are modest at best. So Intent leans on the few things with decent evidence (limits set in advance, friction with an easy way out, if-then plans, batching, stimulus control), labels everything else honestly, and teaches the evidence itself in its Practice lessons rather than promising results.

**Claims Intent avoids:** brain healing or "rewiring", switching off the default mode network, "dopamine resets", manifesting success, curing addiction, "57% less usage", "clinically proven", "better than antidepressants", colours that heal.

Evidence labels used in the app: **Strong** (large randomized trials or meta-analyses), **Good** (well-run experiments or careful field studies, often small), **Early** (pilot studies or single small experiments), **Mixed** (studies disagree), **Real-world example** (what a person says they do; not evidence that it works for everyone).

## Habits

| Finding | Source | What Intent does with it |
|---|---|---|
| 89% of 1,130 filmed phone interactions were started by the person, not a notification; people interacted about every 5 minutes, often without remembering it. | Heitmayer & Lahlou (2021), *Computers in Human Behavior* 116 | The intervention happens at the moment of reaching (the home screen, the pause), not only through notifications. |
| Young adults used phones ~85 times and ~5 hours a day; 55% of uses were under 30 s; estimates didn't match reality. | Andrews, Ellis, Shaw & Piwek (2015), *PLOS ONE* | Honest counts: unlocks, minutes in the apps you want to use less, "Nth session today". |
| Self-control problems account for an estimated 31% of social media use; letting people set future limits reduced use (−22 min/day). | Allcott, Gentzkow & Song (2022), *American Economic Review* | Rules set while calm; loosening a rule waits (cool-off), tightening is instant. |
| A new daily habit took a median of 66 days (range 18–254) to feel automatic; missing a day didn't ruin it. "21 days" is a myth. | Lally et al. (2010), *European Journal of Social Psychology* | No streaks to lose. "Missed a day? Habits survive that." |
| If-then plans had a medium-to-large effect on goal attainment (d = 0.65, 94 tests). | Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006), *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology* | "When I feel the pull, I'll…" plan, shown on the pause screen; the first small step of today's plan, with a when. |
| Hot-cold empathy gap: calm people underestimate future urges. Restraint bias: people most confident in their self-control expose themselves to more temptation and give in more (ex-smokers relapsed more). | Loewenstein (2005), *Health Psychology*; Nordgren, van Harreveld & van der Pligt (2009), *Psychological Science* | Strict mode: limits chosen while calm, no buttons in the moment, looser only from tomorrow, wherever a rule is changed (schedules included). |
| Putting feelings into words reduced amygdala response. | Lieberman et al. (2007), *Psychological Science* | "How are you feeling?" on the pause, with one kind suggestion per answer. Also Dr. Sahar Yousef's advice to intervene at the feeling, before the craving. |
| Dopamine is more about "wanting" than "liking". | Berridge & Robinson (2016), *American Psychologist* | A lesson. Applying it to phones is an interpretation, and we say so. "Dopamine detox" is a misnomer; what helps is stimulus control (removing cues), which is what Intent's tools-only home screen does. |

## What works

| Finding | Source | What Intent does with it |
|---|---|---|
| A short breathing delay before chosen apps: about 36% of attempts were given up, and openings fell 57% over 6 weeks (280 users). That counted *openings*, not minutes; there was no control group; the effect shrank a little as people got used to it (habituation). In a separate randomized experiment, an easy *cancel* option mattered most and a reflective message alone did little. | Grüning, Riedel & Lorenz-Spreen (2023), *PNAS*. Note: field data, early dropouts excluded, app maker co-authored. | The pause screen: one breath (6 s default), then a choice. "Not now" is the biggest button and is pinned at the bottom, always visible. Intent doesn't claim "57% less use". |
| Blocking mobile internet for 2 weeks improved sustained attention (d ≈ 0.23), mental health (≈ 0.56) and well-being (≈ 0.45), 467 people, preregistered. But only about a quarter fully complied. | Castelo et al. (2025), *PNAS Nexus* | Essentials stay instant; only chosen apps get friction. Strict mode is scoped to the narrowest thing that hurts (Shorts and Reels) and time-boxed (a day at a time). |
| Self-set limits cut social media use by about 22 minutes a day (16%); deactivating Facebook for 4 weeks raised well-being by about 0.09 SD. | Allcott, Gentzkow & Song (2022), *American Economic Review*; Allcott et al. (2020), *American Economic Review* | Real but modest effects: the app is described as a tool, not a cure. |
| A bundle of nudges reduced problematic use and screen time in a randomized trial. | Olson et al. (2022), *Int. J. of Mental Health and Addiction* | Several small nudges, each optional. |
| Grayscale cut screen time immediately (N = 112), more than time limits; no immediate change in well-being or grades. A second study: −20 min/day, more sense of control, less stress, unlocks unchanged. | Zimmermann & Sobolev (2023), *Cyberpsychology, Behavior, and Social Networking*; *Mobile Media & Communication* (2024) | Calm, nearly monochrome UI; guide to Android's own grayscale. Not marketed as a brain upgrade. |
| Limiting Facebook, Instagram and Snapchat to 10 min each per day for 3 weeks reduced loneliness and depression (143 students). | Hunt, Marx, Lipson & Young (2018), *J. of Social and Clinical Psychology* | Short default sessions (5 min); optional daily session limits. |

## Notifications and interruptions

| Finding | Source | What Intent does with it |
|---|---|---|
| Receiving a notification, even without interacting, disrupted an attention task. | Stothart, Mitchum & Yehnert (2015), *J. of Experimental Psychology: HPP* | Quiet notifications checklist. Intent itself sends nothing except an optional "your minutes are up" reminder. |
| Alerts on + phone nearby for a week → more inattention and hyperactivity symptoms (221 people). | Kushlev, Proulx & Dunn (2016), *CHI* | Same, plus "park the phone" guidance for focus. |
| Three notification batches a day improved well-being; no notifications at all raised anxiety and FOMO (237 people). | Fitz et al. (2019), *Computers in Human Behavior* | Check-in times for email and work chat, not a total blackout; optionally their notifications wait for the next check-in (calls and alarms never do). |
| Checking email 3×/day lowered daily stress (124 adults, within-person). | Kushlev & Dunn (2015), *Computers in Human Behavior* | Check-in times default to two 20-minute windows (12:30, 18:30). |
| Interruptions during tasks: up to 27% longer, twice the errors, twice the rise in anxiety; deferring to a task boundary helped. | Bailey & Konstan (2006), *Computers in Human Behavior* | Focus sessions; "check messages when you finish a piece of work". |
| Attention residue: switching before finishing leaves part of your attention behind. | Leroy (2009), *OBHDP* | One thing in view; focus sessions. |
| Average time on one screen before switching: ~2.5 min (2004) → ~47 s (2016 on); about half are self-interruptions. | Gloria Mark (2023), *Attention Span*; UC interview (2024) | Today's one thing on the home screen (attention follows goals). |

## Content and attention

| Finding | Source | What Intent does with it |
|---|---|---|
| TikTok-style short videos during a break impaired prospective memory (remembering planned actions); Twitter, YouTube and rest did not (N = 60). | Chiossi et al. (2023), *CHI* | Lesson; default to short sessions for short-video apps. |
| Heavier short-form video use went with weaker cognition (r ≈ −.34) and attention (r ≈ −.38), and with more stress and anxiety. Mostly cross-sectional: it can't show cause. | Nguyen et al. (2025), *Psychological Bulletin* (meta-analysis) | Lesson labeled **Early**, saying what snapshot studies can't show. The experimental Shorts & Reels guard; counts shown as estimates. |
| 2–4 minutes of negative news on Twitter/YouTube lowered mood and optimism; kindness content didn't. | Buchanan, Aknin, Lotun & Sandstrom (2021), *PLOS ONE* | "If you scroll, scroll on purpose" practice. |
| Heavy media multitaskers were worse at filtering distractors; later replications found smaller, inconsistent effects. | Ophir, Nass & Wagner (2009), *PNAS*; later meta-analyses | Lesson labeled **Mixed**. |

Direct replications failed; one meta-analysis found a small cost, a larger one (33 studies, 4,368 people) found no overall effect. | Ward et al. (2017); Ruiz Pardo & Minda (2022); Böttger et al. (2023); Hartanto et al. (2024) | Labeled **Mixed**. We still recommend "out of reach", because within reach the phone gets used more (Heitmayer). |
| A phone on the table or in hand during a café conversation → less fulfilling conversation, less perceived empathy (100 pairs). | Misra, Cheng, Genevie & Yuan (2016), *Environment and Behavior* | Lesson. |
| Phones out during a meal with friends → more distraction, less enjoyment (300+ people, randomized). | Dwyer, Kushlev & Dunn (2018), *J. of Experimental Social Psychology* | Lesson. |

## Planning, motivation and the mind

| Finding | Source | What Intent does with it |
|---|---|---|
| Pairing a wish with its obstacle and an if-then plan (mental contrasting with implementation intentions, "WOOP") helped goal attainment, g ≈ 0.34 across about 20 studies, possibly inflated by publication bias. A self-guided digital version did no better than control in one trial. | Wang et al. (2021), *Frontiers in Psychology* (meta-analysis); Oettingen (2014), *Rethinking Positive Thinking*; Mutter et al. (2020) | "What matters today?": one thing, the first small step and when, and optionally the obstacle and an if-then plan. Short, optional, never graded. |
| Positive fantasies about a goal predicted less effort and worse results (job search, weight loss, exams); they lowered energy (systolic blood pressure). | Oettingen & Mayer (2002), *J. of Personality and Social Psychology*; Kappes & Oettingen (2011), *J. of Experimental Social Psychology* | No "visualize your success" prompts. A lesson: "Dreaming of the finish line can drain you." |
| Picturing the *process* (where, when, how you'll study) beat picturing the *outcome* (the grade): students studied about 16 hours instead of 12 and did better. | Taylor, Pham, Rivkin & Armor (1998), *American Psychologist*; Pham & Taylor (1999) | The morning plan asks you to picture the first step, not the trophy. |
| Belief in manifesting (The Secret, the "law of attraction") went with expecting more success, risky investing and a higher chance of having gone bankrupt. Correlational. | Dixon, Hornsey & Hartley (2023), *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin* | A myth-check lesson. Intent never says thinking makes it so. |
| After a failure, self-compassion led to more effort to improve than self-esteem boosting or nothing. | Breines & Chen (2012), *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin* | The evening look-back meets "Not today" with "Slips are part of changing a habit. What will you do next time the urge shows up?" Shame-based lines are off by default. |
| "I am a lovable person" made people with low self-esteem feel worse. Affirming your values has a better (though mixed) record. | Wood, Perunovic & Lee (2009), *Psychological Science*; Cohen & Sherman (2014), *Annual Review of Psychology* | No "I am…" affirmations. A lesson explains why. |
| Writing a specific to-do list for the coming days before bed: fell asleep about 9 minutes faster than writing about finished tasks (57 adults). | Scullin et al. (2018), *J. of Experimental Psychology: General* | The look-back ends with tomorrow's first step, offered back the next morning. |
| Expressive writing and gratitude exercises have small average effects. | Frattaroli (2006), *Psychological Bulletin*; Davis et al. (2016), *J. of Counseling Psychology* | The look-back is brief and optional; no daily writing duty, no journaling streak. |
| Five minutes a day of cyclic sighing (two inhales, a long exhale) improved positive mood more than mindfulness meditation over a month (108 people). | Balban et al. (2023), *Cell Reports Medicine* | One option in the one-minute reset, labeled **Early**. Non-breathing options too, for people who find breath focus uncomfortable. |
| Mindfulness meditation programs: moderate evidence of small improvements in anxiety (≈ 0.38) and depression (≈ 0.30); not better than other active treatments. | Goyal et al. (2014), *JAMA Internal Medicine* | "Meditation helps, a little" lesson, with a note to see a doctor or therapist when struggling. |
| Urge surfing: smokers who practised it smoked fewer cigarettes, though their urges stayed as strong. | Bowen & Marlatt (2009), *Psychology of Addictive Behaviors* | "An urge can be present without opening the app." The reset and the plan for the pull. |

## Design and accessibility

- Text meets WCAG 2.1 AA contrast (4.5:1) on every surface it's drawn on, the faintest gray included; everything you can tap is at least 48 dp (Android's own guidance).
- With Android's animations turned off (reduce motion), the pause circle stays still and says "Breathe naturally" instead of pacing your breath; nothing on any screen moves forever.
- There's no evidence that particular colours heal or calm; the palette is chosen for legibility and quiet, not therapy. No red shame counters.
- One quote a day instead of a rotating feed of them, and no endless "next": a quote is something to read, not something to scroll.

## People (real-world examples, not evidence)

- **Warren Buffett** used a basic flip phone until 2020 and says he uses his iPhone mostly for calls (CNBC, 2020).
- **Bill Gates** gave his children phones at 14, kept phones off the dinner table, and is known for "Think Weeks" (The Mirror, 2017; WSJ, 2005).
- **Steve Jobs** limited technology at home (Nick Bilton, *The New York Times*, 2014).
- **Tim Cook** "gutted" his notifications after reading his Screen Time data (2018–2023 interviews).
- **Cal Newport** says he has never had a social media account; *Digital Minimalism* (2019).
- **Tristan Harris** / Center for Humane Technology: notifications only from people, tools-only home screen, launch other apps by typing, charge outside the bedroom.
- **Pavel Durov** has said he kept his phone on airplane mode or mute, no longer carries one day to day, and avoids screens in the morning (Lex Fridman podcast; Tucker Carlson interview, 2025).
- **Christopher Nolan** doesn't use email or own a smartphone; he says it gives him time to think (Hollywood Reporter, 2014; 2026 interviews).

## Android facts the design respects

- A launcher controls its own home screen. Apps opened from notifications, recents or links bypass it unless an accessibility service (Focus Guard) notices them. Intent says this plainly.
- Focus Guard listens only to `typeWindowStateChanged` with `canRetrieveWindowContent="false"`: it's content-blind.
- The Shorts & Reels guard is a separate service limited to YouTube and Instagram. It needs `canRetrieveWindowContent="true"` and `flagReportViewIds` to find the Shorts or Reels player by its view names; it never reads text, and its swipe counts are estimates.
- Background activity starts are allowed for bound accessibility services, which is how the pause screens appear.
- Unlocking can resume the previous app instead of the home screen, so the unlock check-in is shown when home is actually visible, and Focus Guard re-checks the app in front after unlocking.
- Android 13+ restricts accessibility for sideloaded apps until the user allows "restricted settings".
- Bluetooth, Wi-Fi, hotspot and mobile data are device services. Intent never touches them; swipe down opens quick settings.

## What we decided not to build (yet)

- **More than view names from inside apps**: the Shorts & Reels guard stays experimental because it relies on YouTube's and Instagram's view names, which change with app updates. It reads nothing else. The whole-app pause and short sessions remain the dependable baseline.
- **AI coaching or "productivity scores"** for your content: privacy cost, no demonstrated benefit.
- **Streaks, leaderboards, motivational notifications**: they create new reasons to check the phone.
- **A VPN or network filter**: one active VPN per device, fragile, and unnecessary for the core idea.
